## Supplementary Notes

### Supplementary Note 1: Data-quality review and baseline code patches

A reproducibility review preceded all comparisons reported in the main text. We describe here the four classes of data repairs, the patches applied to the four baseline codebases, and the two decoding/scoring defects that the review exposed in our own harness. All repairs are scripted, all software versions are pinned, and every benchmark number in Fig. 2 of the main text and in Supplementary Table 2 postdates these repairs. The composition of the cleaned benchmark is summarised in Supplementary Figure 1.

**Multi-copy crystal chains and the canonical truth set.** Forty crystal chains were stored in the source pipeline as concatenated tandem copies of the same molecule (for example, 1EI2_A as 425 nt = 17 × 25 nt and 2KGP_A as 250 nt = 10 × 25 nt), among them three chains of the cluster-based test set whose native sequences were therefore spurious concatenations. We rebuilt all 104 finetuning-pool-specific chains as single-copy all-atom PDBs (same-key consecutive grouping, with a non-adjacent repeat marking the start of the next copy) and regenerated the canonical truth set (`canonical_v2`, 6,346 chains) under the same logic; every evaluation reported in this paper scores against single-copy canonical natives. The pre-repair stage-1 pretraining run had seen approximately 37 of the concatenated sequences (≤ 1.2% of its training data); this is recorded here for completeness and the model was not retrained, because the contamination affects the training distribution only, not any test truth.

**Protein contamination and residue-name normalisation.** Twenty-nine chains listed as RNA in the source pool were in fact protein-backbone traces (from entries 1C51, 1ILX, 4WWE, 4WWT, 6O7H and others); all 29 lay inside the training split and were removed from the baseline training sets (`canonical_v2_dropped.json`). Modified nucleotides were mapped to their parent bases under a documented mapping (`resname_to_char.json`); the ambiguous CCD residue name `N` and 5′-variant names such as `+U` were normalised analogously, and residues without a mapping do not enter any sequence. Legacy atom names (`O5*` for `O5′`) were normalised in 13 chains (4 in the cluster-based test set, 9 in training). Positions whose native base is undetermined (N) are excluded from every denominator in this paper.

**Patches to baseline codebases.** Running the four baselines (RhoDesign, gRNAde, NA-MPNN and AlignIF; Supplementary Refs. S15–S18) locally required source-level patches, all of which are documented in the released repository. (i) AlignIF: the inference FASTA writer reused the loop variable `seq`, so that on chains with PDB numbering gaps only the first of 20 samples had its placeholder underscores removed — 19 of 20 samples per such chain carried invalid `-` padding; we patched the writer (new `out_seq` variable) and reran inference in full. A second AlignIF patch maps multi-character residue names (DU, PSU and similar) that crashed sequence assembly to parent bases, and a third repairs an `argparse` `type=bool` defect that silently forced `--use_msta` on (no numerical effect on our no-MStA runs). (ii) NA-MPNN: we patched four failure modes of the official parsing stack — residues missing C1′ coordinates are silently dropped, insertion-code variants broke a unique-resnum counting step (`pdbutils.py`, mirrored in `na_data_utils.py`), uncommon residue names lacked fallback mappings, and optional `openbabel`/CIF-library dependencies were made degradable. Five test chains whose source PDBs lack C1′ coordinates (5NFV_B, 7E9I_A, 8U5J_A, 8VPV_A, 9GUT_X) cannot be repaired at the data level and are evaluated on valid positions only (difflib-aligned equal blocks; Methods of the main text). (iii) RhoDesign: NMR multi-model entries (2KGP_A, 6IZP_A) were initially fed to the model as ten-model concatenations; both chains were rerun against the canonical single-copy inputs.

**The gRNAde decoding repair.** A native-likelihood diagnostic, run while assembling the perplexity column of Supplementary Table 2, exposed a vocabulary-ordering defect in our gRNAde inference harness: sampled tokens had been written out with the alphabet order A,C,G,U, whereas the model's training pipeline defines the token order A,G,C,U (`tools/gRNAde/src/constants.py`), so C and G had been interchanged at decode time (A and U are unaffected). Three independent checks established the correct order. Teacher-forced scoring of the native sequence under the retrained checkpoint favours the official order on 5 of 5 spot-checked chains (for 1D4R_A, −0.970 against −1.121 log units per nucleotide). A paired 40-chain × 100-sample comparison at t = 0.1 gives a mean per-chain recovery advantage of +0.0199 for the official decode (25 wins, 12 losses, 3 ties). And the two decodes are related by a deterministic bijection, so the official sequences could be reconstructed exactly by interchanging C and G in the existing pools. Recomputing the cluster-based layer raised gRNAde recovery from 50.15% to 53.00% (best sample per chain) and from 31.21% to 35.77% (t = 0.1 mean), and every downstream metric was recomputed under the corrected pools (structural self-consistency 0.219 → 0.265 scTM; contact fidelity 0.391 → 0.472 scMCC; family-profile KL 4.61 → 4.66). The repair only raises gRNAde's scores, and every claim of a TACo-Aptamer advantage over gRNAde is unchanged after it (all P ≤ 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁷). The pre-repair values are retained as a labelled reference row in the released per-chain files.

**Scoring-geometry repair in the interaction-analysis harness.** The same review caught a second defect on our own side: the nine-atom featurization used for likelihood scoring in this analysis (Fig. 6d of the main text; the GVP encoder input representation, Supplementary Ref. S24) had filled the third coordinate slot — defined at training time as the literal N1 atom of every base — with the glycosidic N9 atom of purines, erasing the geometric signal that distinguishes purines from pyrimidines and shifting the scoring geometry off the training distribution. A 2 × 2 decomposition on 40 AptaDB chains (N1/N9 geometry × the two scoring checkpoints) showed that the initial, inverted result (aptamers scoring below matched random controls) was entirely due to the geometry: N1 geometry gives +2.22 and +2.11 log units for the two checkpoints, while N9 geometry gives −0.42 and −0.45. All interaction-analysis numbers in the main text and in Supplementary Note 4 were produced after this repair.

### Supplementary Note 2: Zero-shot ligand identities and ligand-family definitions

This note defines the zero-shot ligand split and the ligand families used in
the leave-one-family-out (LOFO) analysis, and states the attribution rules
that map PDB-deposited ligands onto the chains of our corpus.

**Zero-shot split composition.** The zero-shot test layer contains 93 chains
(54 from the cluster test partition, 39 from the independent partition) whose
bound ligands have zero CCD-code overlap with any ligand in the training or
fine-tuning corpus. 88 of the 93 chains carry an attributed ligand; the
remaining five (5NFV_B, 6IV6_G, 6VRD_B, 8SWB_B, 8SWC_B) have no deposited
ligand under our attribution rules and are retained as apo controls. The 88
attributed chains span 43 distinct CCD ligand codes; Supplementary Table 3
lists all 43 codes with full chemical names, per-ligand chain counts, and an
example chain. The most frequent are AR6 (an ADP derivative; 14 chains), NI4
(6), XAN (xanthine; 6), HZG (4), NCA (nicotinamide; 4), QIJ (4), MMC
(methylmercury ion; 3), V7U (3), and V8C (3); 14 ligands occur on two chains
and 20 on a single chain, including 141 (oxypurinol), DHF (dihydrofolate),
N2P (pentane-1,5-diamine), RIV (rivaroxaban), and MIX (a mitoxantrone-class
compound).

**Training-corpus ligand vocabulary.** The 768 training chains (510 PDB
entries) carry 247 distinct CCD codes. Of these, 242 have usable Morgan
fingerprints (Supplementary Ref. S7); five codes (6NO, 6O1, B12, B1Z, HEM) plus the zero-shot code
OXV fail RDKit (Supplementary Ref. S6) parsing and are excluded from fingerprint statistics. SMILES
strings are taken from the CCD `components.cif` SMILES_CANONICAL field
(CACTVS) preferentially.

**Fingerprint similarity.** Of the 88 attributed zero-shot chains, 87 enter
the similarity analysis (the OXV ligand of 6PQ7_C has no parseable SMILES).
The distribution of per-chain maximum Tanimoto similarity to any training
ligand has median 0.379, and exactly 1/87 chains reaches ≥ 0.9 (maximum
observed 1.0, an identical fingerprint). These values underlie the similarity
axis of Fig. 6 of the main text.

**Ligand-attribution rules.** Ligands were attributed to chains at the PDB
level by a five-stage cascade, in order: (i) element_exact — author-deposited
ligand–chain pairing (383 of 510 entries); (ii) element_union — union of
symmetry-related ligand copies (23); (iii) greedy_pdb — geometry-based pairing
at ≥ 0.8 contact overlap (8); (iv) greedy_ext — geometry-based pairing
requiring ≥ 4 heavy-atom contacts (34); (v) loose_pdb — relaxed geometric
pairing (62). Cross-validation against manual annotation on 115
interface-annotated chains agrees in 112 cases (97.4%); the three
disagreements are multi-copy partial-deposition cases (1OLN, and 5D8H where
QUA is re-attributed to NO1, and 8GS2 where C5P is re-attributed to XY7).

**LOFO family definitions.** Ligand families were defined at the CCD level and
curated for chemical coherence (Supplementary Table 6): aminoglycoside =
{AM2, GET, NMY, PAR, RIO, TOB} (128 chains, 70 PDB entries); sam_sah =
{SAH, SAM} (55 chains, 42 entries); cla = {CLA} (24 chains, 2 entries); ntp =
{ATP, CTP, GTP, UTP} (20 chains, 15 entries); 2ba = {2BA} (16 chains, 16
entries); a sixth family, gai (15 chains, 6 entries), was defined but not
included in the top-five LOFO rotation; flavins = {FAD, FMN} (8 chains) were
considered and excluded for size. Removing each family from the training pool
leaves 640, 713, 744, 748, and 752 chains respectively; the only-k-family
counterpart pools contain 128 (k = 1), 183 (k = 2), 207 (k = 3), and 243
(k = 5) chains, and the full-control pool 768 chains.

### Supplementary Note 3: Conditioning-channel diagnostics

This note collects the three diagnostics we ran to test whether the model's
performance gain over baselines can be attributed to the conditioning channels
(secondary structure, ligand identity, interface contacts; ligand-identity conditioning is the RNA-side analogue of LigandMPNN's atomic-context conditioning on proteins, Supplementary Ref. S14) rather than to
confounds of the evaluation protocol. All scores in this note were produced by
the published ensemble (ens2, 300 samples per chain, seed 42) under the
canonical 477-chain cluster hold-out, unless stated otherwise.

**Protocol validation.** Before interpreting any per-chain statistic, we
verified that the survey-time scoring environment reproduces the training-time
one. Comparing re-scored per-chain log probabilities against the archived
step-77 design pools, 8 of 477 chains deviated beyond numerical tolerance; the
divergence is attributable to a `torch_scatter` compilation difference between
the two environments and has mean absolute deviation of 0.019 in per-chain
best recovery among the affected chains. Recomputing the headline metrics in
the survey environment gives 0.7464 (best) and 0.5970 (t = 0.1 mean), which fall
inside the bootstrap confidence intervals of the canonical main-table values
(0.749 / 0.604; Table 1 of the main text). All conditioning diagnostics below
therefore use the survey-time scoring path, and figures derived from this note
carry the 8-chain deviation as a stated caveat.

**Secondary structure robustness.** We re-scored all 477 chains under four
secondary structure (SS) conditions: the zeroSS control (all-unpaired SS
string), a sequence-shuffled control (shuffle seed 9701, dinucleotide-shuffled
sequence scored against the same backbone), and the centroidSS variant
(centroid of the RNAfold ensemble instead of the MFE structure; ViennaRNA, Supplementary Ref. S3). Relative to
the standard condition, zeroSS changes per-chain best recovery by −0.28%
(95% CI −0.55 to +0.00) and t = 0.1 mean recovery by −0.46% (CI −0.62 to −0.30);
shuffleSS by −0.26% [−0.57, +0.05] and −0.59% [−0.77, −0.43]; centroidSS by
+0.17% [−0.09, +0.44] and +0.002% [−0.08, +0.08]. The inference-time SS
channel is therefore remarkably inert: replacing it with a structurally
meaningless string, or pairing it with a shuffled sequence, costs less than
0.6 percentage point. This robustness is consistent with the training-time
ablations: removing the SS embedding costs −3.28% best recovery in the
quick-30 protocol (Supplementary Table 7a) but only −0.83% under the release
recipe scored with the 300-sample pool (Supplementary Table 7b), indicating
that the channel acts as a mild regulariser rather than a dominant signal
source. We interpret the SS channel as providing a coarse structural prior
that the backbone geometry largely subsumes at inference time.

**Interface-conditioned scoring (teacher forcing).** On the 115 chains with a
usable annotated interface (Methods of the main text and Supplementary Note 2;
905 interface positions), we compared teacher-forced per-residue
log probabilities of the native sequence between the full model and an
interface-masked variant. The mean difference between interface and
non-interface positions under the published ensemble is −0.0003 nats (95% CI
[−0.0020, +0.0013]; one-sided permutation p = 0.879; 31.3% of chains
positive). The same comparison under shuffleSS gives −0.0003 (p = 0.888), and
under the seed-43 single model −0.0018 (p = 0.99994). We also computed the
Spearman correlation between per-position decoder attention to ligand tokens
and the physical C1′–ligand distance; the per-chain mean correlation is
+0.104 (median 0.257, with 33% of chains negative), i.e. attention does not
systematically focus on the nearest ligand atoms. Together these results show
that the model does not rely on ligand–nucleotide contact patterns
at the level of individual positions.

**Pocket-injection test.** We next asked whether supplying a *bona fide*
binding pocket at inference time improves design of the interface residues.
On the same 115 chains we compared three conditions (30-sample pools): the
true pocket, an empty-pocket control, and a no-ligand placebo. Scoring with
the true pocket against the empty pocket changes recovery by +1.04 percentage
points (95% CI −1.1 to +3.1; permutation p = 0.16, not significant), while the
no-ligand placebo — a ligand token stream fed to a model variant trained
without ligand conditioning — collapses recovery by −15.6 points, confirming
that the ligand pathway is causally engaged even though the true-pocket gain
is small. Because pocket placement could itself inject noise, we upgraded the
pocket geometry to an intrafamily transplant: the ligand pose was transferred
by C1′ Kabsch superposition from the nearest same-family structure (median
centroid displacement 0.85 Å, 90th percentile 2.25 Å). Under this upgraded
geometry the true-versus-geometrically-transplanted interface log probability
difference remains at about +0.0002 nats (not significant), ruling out placement
noise as the explanation for the small effect size.

Taken together, the diagnostics support the interpretation in the main text:
the conditioning channels are used — ablating them degrades both overall and
interface recovery (Supplementary Table 7b) — but the published model
distributes its competence across backbone geometry and sequence context
rather than depending on any single channel, which explains the robustness of
the headline metrics to SS perturbation and the modest marginal value of exact
pocket geometry on the native-backbone benchmark.

### Supplementary Note 4: Analysis of measured aptamer–target interactions

This note describes the assembly of the aptamer cohort and the
scoring protocol behind Fig. 6d,e of the main text.

**Cohort assembly.** We scraped 1,350 aptamer–target records from AptaDB (Supplementary Ref. S19; 283
RNA, 1,067 DNA entries; target classes: 639 protein, 393 small molecule, 189
cell, 129 other) and applied the quality filters described in Methods
(reported Kd, sequence validity, target identity), yielding 254 unique
RNA–target interactions spanning 139 targets. From the UTexas aptamer
collection (Supplementary Ref. S20) we took the 1,495-row spreadsheet and
applied identical filters, yielding 228 interactions; the merged cohort
contains 442 interactions. To exclude leakage from the TACo-Aptamer training
corpus, we clustered all aptamer sequences together with the training chains
using CD-HIT-EST (Supplementary Ref. S1) at 0.8 sequence identity and removed every aptamer sharing a
cluster with a training sequence. This removed 40 entries (including 3 exact
duplicates of training sequences), leaving 214 clean AptaDB interactions; the
full 254-entry set is retained only as a sensitivity analysis. No UTexas entry
was removed by this screen.

**Scoring protocol.** Every aptamer sequence was folded with RhoFold+ (Supplementary Ref. S4; 254/254
successful, zero failures) and scored teacher-forced under the published ens2
ensemble. Two random-sequence controls share the evaluation burden: randA
pools ten random sequences matched for length scored against the same predicted
structure and the same secondary structure annotation as the real aptamer,
and randB uses random sequences each with their own secondary structure
annotation. The primary statistic is the per-aptamer log-likelihood advantage
ΔlogP = logP(real) − mean logP(randA).

**Results.** On the merged clean cohort the mean advantage is +1.95 log units,
and 442/442 aptamers (100%) score above their randA control (fraction
negative = 0.0). Stratified by source: AptaDB clean (n = 214) +2.01 [95% CI
1.94, 2.09] (sensitivity analysis on the full n = 254 set: +2.03); UTexas
(n = 228) +1.90 [1.80, 2.00]. These values underlie Fig. 6d of the main text.

**Affinity-ranked families.** For the 19 families with at least three measured
Kd values, the mean Spearman correlation between model log-likelihood and
measured affinity is ρ = 0.11 (95% CI −0.15 to 0.36; median 0.22; 58% of
families positive; not significant). The sensitivity set of 38 families with
at least two Kd values gives a consistent picture. Three secondary analyses
accompany the headline correlation: (i) the best-affinity aptamer of each
family sits at mean rank percentile 0.439 [0.28, 0.61] of the model's scores
(one-sided p = 0.999 against a top-heavy null); (ii) after z-standardisation within each family and
pooling (n = 84 pairs), the pooled correlation is
ρ = 0.046 and the top affinity quartile scores +0.20 SD above the bottom
quartile; (iii) an exploratory top-half sensitivity analysis over 10 families
gives 0.433 [0.267, 0.600] (one-sided p = 0.0039 against the 0.5 null). Analysis (iii) is exploratory: the archived pre-repair report stated 0.633
(p = 0.002) under an earlier scoring configuration, and the post-repair value
above has not yet been independently re-confirmed; we therefore make no claim
from analysis (iii) beyond the qualitative direction (⚠️PENDING analyst
confirmation of the final configuration).

**Confounder diagnostics.** Four diagnostics accompany the signal. D1: a length scan of random sequences shows a residual +0.003 log
units per nucleotide length dependence after the scoring fix (compared with +0.054
before the fix), i.e. the length bias that inflated the original analysis is
largely removed. D2: a borrowed-structure control (scoring aptamers against
unrelated structures) degrades the signal, qualitatively confirming that the
advantage is structure-dependent rather than sequence-composition-dependent.
D3: GC content correlates with model log-likelihood at ρ = 0.02 after the fix
(−0.116 before), so base composition does not confound the ranking. D4:
excluding annotated G-quadruplex aptamers (which RhoFold+ folds poorly) leaves
the qualitative conclusions unchanged.

**Relation to the N9 geometry repair.** An earlier internal version of this
analysis, run under the pre-repair backbone geometry, produced a null or
negative signal (N9-geometry pool: −0.42/−0.45 log units by
source, compared with +2.22/+2.11 under the repaired N1 geometry; Supplementary
Note 1). The signal therefore depends on the same
backbone-geometry correctness that underlies the structure benchmarks, and we
report it only for the repaired pipeline.

### Supplementary Note 5: Contamination screen of the RNet training corpus

This note documents the leakage audit that led us to exclude the RNet-derived
signal from every figure and table of the main text. The audited corpus is the training set of RNet (Supplementary Ref. S22), the chemical-mapping model that guided the OpenKnot pseudoknot-design challenge (Supplementary Ref. S23).

**Corpus.** We downloaded the five RMDB releases (Supplementary Ref. S21) from the DasLab GitHub mirror
(12.5 GB, CC0), parsed 1,024 RDAT files, and reconstructed 4,525,354
sequences, including 8,868 mutants encoded in the M2 format (reference base,
position, and mutant base resolved against the construct OFFSET). After
deduplication, 422 native chemical-mapping constructs were retained, layered
as 255 test, 130 independent, and 37 zero-shot constructs (median test
construct length 43 nt).

**Matching protocol.** Candidate matches between the 422 constructs and the
TACo-Aptamer training/validation chains were pre-screened by shared 12-mer
content (2-bit encoded pairwise counting; constructs sharing ≥2 12-mers with
any corpus chain advanced). Candidates were then aligned with edlib (Supplementary Ref. S9) under
three tiers: (i) exact match after U/T normalisation; (ii) global identity
≥ 0.8; (iii) semi-global (HW) embedding with edit distance ≤ 10% of construct
length, counted in both directions. A separate "fragment suspicion" flag
(≥6 shared 12-mers without an alignment hit) was logged for reporting only and
never counted as a hit.

**Verdict.** The screen returns FAIL. Zero constructs match exactly; 11
constructs match at high identity (tier ii); 227 constructs are embedded
within corpus sequences (tier iii). Counting tiers additively as in the
analysis log, 238 of 422 constructs (56%) are flagged: 186/255 (73%) of the
test layer, 27/130 (21%) of the independent layer, and 25/37 (68%) of the
zero-shot layer; a further 284 fragment-suspicion records are not counted.
Because all 11 high-identity matches are also embedded matches, the
deduplicated union of flagged constructs is 227/422 (53.8%; test layer
178/255, 69.8%) — the main text cites this audit by the deduplicated union
(54% of natives; 70% cluster-based, 21% time-based), and the additive per-tier
counting above is given for completeness. Example hits
include construct 1Y26_X, which aligns at 0.96–0.99 identity to training
chains ADDRSW_1M7_* and ADD71_STD with about 60 shared 12-merss. The contamination
is intrinsic to the chemical-mapping corpus (the RMDB constructs are
themselves derived from well-studied RNAs that populate the PDB), not
created by our split.

**Consequence.** Because no decontaminated split of the RNet corpus exists
that is both disjoint from our training data and large enough to score, the
RNet signal is excluded from every figure and table of the main text and of
this supplement. The audit artifacts are archived in
`data/survey/89_rnet_leakage_audit.json`.

### Supplementary Note 6: The OpenKnot challenge and the scope of structure-only design

A concurrent result helps fix the limits of what structure-only design can reach. In the OpenKnot challenge (Supplementary Ref. S23), deep-learning designers matched experienced human experts on de novo pseudoknot targets without relying on 3D structure prediction, guided by RNet (Supplementary Ref. S22), a foundation model trained on roughly two million prior chemical-mapping measurements from Eterna. Two aspects of that result bound its relevance here. First, OpenKnot probed every design in the absence of small-molecule ligands so that pseudoknot formation would reflect the design alone. The objective was the correct secondary structure, not binding, and no participating framework conditioned on a target molecule. Second, although the best designs formed well-ordered folds, the cryo-EM structures revealed noncanonical tertiary interactions that were neither designed by the AI methods nor predicted by AlphaFold 3 (Supplementary Ref. S26). Design centred on secondary structure controls topology while leaving the tertiary geometry of the pocket, which defines binding, unspecified. For aptamers that geometry is the specification, so design from secondary structure alone and atomic target conditioning are complements rather than substitutes. Our contamination screen adds one further caution. When the Eterna chemical-mapping libraries that trained RNet were scanned against our benchmark, near-verbatim copies of test-layer native sequences (global identity ≥ 0.8, or embedding in a longer measured construct at ≤ 10% edit distance) had already been measured for 54% of natives, 70% of the cluster-based layer but only 21% of the time-based layer (Supplementary Note 5, Supplementary Table 10). Contamination of this kind is invisible to sequence-level checks against structural databases. The pattern validates time-based holdout as a principle of split design, and cautions that structure-prediction test sets cannot benchmark such models without explicit contamination screening.

### Supplementary Note 7: Ensemble selection, weight-soup controls and the validation record

**Variant scan.** The released model is an ensemble of two stage-2 checkpoints selected on validation data. Candidate members came from a scan of development variants — random seed, secondary structure (SS) dropout rate, batch size, exponential moving average (EMA) of weights, and exact reruns of the base recipe — all finetuned under one identical stage-2 recipe (joint 1:1 mixing with stage-1 chains against forgetting, validation-based checkpoint selection, batch size 4, 8 epochs) and evaluated on the cluster-based test layer with pools of 300 samples (Supplementary Table 11; scan numbers use the pre-canonical manifest native caliber, which differs by at most 0.1% from the canonical caliber of Supplementary Table 2). The scan ranked the seed-43 variant and the SS-dropout-0.15 variant as the two strongest single models (71.49% and 71.45% for the best sample per chain; all other pre-selection variants ≤ 70.95%). The two differ in both seed and SS-dropout regularisation and occupy different stage-1 basins of the loss landscape. A later follow-up variant combining both changes (seed 43 × SS-dropout 0.15) reached 71.44% for the best sample; it was trained after the member selection had been frozen and did not alter it, and we report it in Supplementary Table 11. Training and ensemble decoding are implemented with the fairseq toolkit (Supplementary Ref. S25).

**Output-space ensembling and the validation record.** The ensemble averages the two members' stepwise softmax probability distributions before multinomial sampling — the standard ensemble-decoding operation for autoregressive sequence models, i.e. a linear opinion pool of the two stepwise distributions (Supplementary Refs. S11, S12). Pool sizes are unchanged relative to single models and the inference cost doubles, as reported in Extended Data Table 4 of the main text. Ensemble error is bounded by the mean member error minus a diversity (ambiguity) term (Supplementary Ref. S10), so gains are expected to grow precisely when members disagree. A three-member ensemble adding the third-ranked variant scored marginally higher on the cluster-based test set (75.2% against 74.6% for the best sample, manifest caliber) but was rejected on validation (validation best 0.8256 against 0.8263; validation t = 0.1 mean 0.7256 against 0.7331; the simpler two-member configuration was preferred), and test-set numbers were not used for the final configuration choice. After the interface-weighting removal (Methods of the main text), the selection was re-verified on the same 96 validation chains with the final noiface checkpoints: ens2 0.8281 > ssdo15 0.8135 > seed43 0.7954 (validation best sample; t = 0.1 means 0.7384, 0.7437, 0.7343). The best single model already outperforms every baseline on every test layer under both recovery measures, so the ensemble is not load-bearing for any claim in the main text; its own contribution is quantified in Fig. 4a of the main text (+3.2% globally, +4.3% at the interface) and in Supplementary Table 7.

**Weight-soup controls.** Weight-space averaging across these training variants degrades or fails to help: soups of two to four checkpoints scored 66.17–70.90% on the best sample, against 71.5% for the best single model (Supplementary Table 11). Averaging two stage-2 checkpoints that share one stage-1 basin leaves performance unchanged (70.90%), adding the SS-dropout variant degrades it (69.64%), and adding the seed-43 variant across stage-1 basins degrades it sharply (66.17%). This is the behaviour predicted by linear mode connectivity theory (Supplementary Ref. S13): independently trained networks are not connected by linear paths in weight space, so weight averaging can only succeed within a basin, whereas probability-space averaging does not require connectivity and is the operation that exploits between-basin diversity.

### Supplementary Note 8: Baseline retraining and inference protocol

**Shared splits and effective training sets.** RhoDesign, gRNAde, NA-MPNN and AlignIF were retrained from their official training procedures on the identical 5,773-chain training split (validation on the same 96 chains for every method, TACo-Aptamer included). Each official featurization pipeline tolerates a slightly different subset of the split, and we report the effective training-set sizes rather than forcing a common subset: TACo-Aptamer 5,773 (the custom featurization is the most tolerant), AlignIF 5,733 (official processed-format filtering), RhoDesign 5,683 (coordinate-completeness requirements), NA-MPNN 5,679 (official parsing, after removal of the 29 protein-trace chains of Supplementary Note 1) and gRNAde 5,584 (biotite base-pair extraction fails on some short or aberrant chains). These sizes are also listed in Extended Data Table 1 of the main text.

**Official procedures and checkpoint selection.** RhoDesign was trained with a custom loop around the official model code, selecting the checkpoint `best_val.pth` by validation recovery. gRNAde was trained with the official `main.py` loop on the official-style three-file inputs, selecting `best_checkpoint.h5` by validation. NA-MPNN was trained with official hyperparameters (hidden dimension 128, 3 + 3 encoder/decoder layers, k = 32 neighbours, Noam schedule, label smoothing 0.1, automatic mixed precision, BATCH_TOKENS 6000, TOTAL_STEPS 20000 run to completion); all 21 saved checkpoints were evaluated on validation and `s_11016.pt` was selected (epoch 153, validation top-1 accuracy 0.6771). AlignIF was trained for the full 80 epochs of its official schedule (validation loss 0.880 → 0.697) and the epoch-79 checkpoint was used.

**Inference pools and scoring conventions.** For benchmark inference, every method conditions only on the backbone and the predicted secondary structure — TACo-Aptamer included, the ligand context having acted during finetuning (see Supplementary Note 3 for the inference-time pocket-injection control). Pools follow the official inference defaults of each method at identical intent: three temperatures (0.1, 0.5, 1.0) × 100 sequences for TACo-Aptamer, RhoDesign and gRNAde, with RhoDesign down-sampling long chains per its official rule (50 per temperature above 200 nt, 30 above 400 nt); t = 0.1 × 20 for AlignIF (run without MStA retrieval, matching the input parity of the other methods) and t = 0.1 × 10 for NA-MPNN. Comparing best samples therefore mildly disadvantages the two small-pool methods, and the t = 0.1 mean is reported alongside precisely to control for pool size (Fig. 2b,c of the main text). Outputs shorter than the native sequence (the five NA-MPNN chains of Supplementary Note 1; AlignIF positions returned as dashes) are aligned by equal-block matching and scored on valid positions only, with N positions excluded from all denominators. The identical protocol was carried over unchanged to the predicted-backbone benchmark (Supplementary Figure 8; Supplementary Table 8) and the efficiency probes (Supplementary Figure 9; Supplementary Table 9).

The supplementary figures collect the chain-level and family-level characterisations of these benchmark pools: cross-method confidence coupling (Supplementary Figure 2), native-sequence likelihood (Supplementary Figure 3), ligand-family stratification (Supplementary Figure 4), the fingerprint robustness of the zero-shot advantage (Supplementary Figure 5; Supplementary Table 4), the family-holdout retraining experiments (Supplementary Table 5), interface consensus composition (Supplementary Figure 6), the two fold-fidelity axes (Supplementary Figure 7), predicted-backbone transfer (Supplementary Figure 8; Supplementary Table 8), inference efficiency (Supplementary Figure 9; Supplementary Table 9), the temperature–diversity scan (Supplementary Figure 10) and the conditioning-channel perturbations detailed in Supplementary Note 3 (Supplementary Figure 11).
