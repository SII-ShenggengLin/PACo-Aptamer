## Abstract

RNA aptamers are selected to bind molecular targets, but computational aptamer design has remained structure-only: the small-molecule ligand never enters the model, and improved structural fidelity need not improve binding. Here we present TACo-Aptamer, which brings the target inside RNA inverse folding. Ligand pocket atoms are embedded as tokens and jointly attended alongside geometric backbone representations and a predicted secondary structure; the model is pretrained on RNA-only structures, then finetuned on RNA–ligand complexes. Because the few percent of positions at the ligand interface are invisible to global metrics, we introduce a benchmark with three test layers: cluster-based holdout, time-based splitting and, for the first time, ligand holdout (test ligands unseen in training); all methods are retrained on identical data. TACo-Aptamer outperforms every baseline on every test set, generalises to chemically unseen ligands — with the advantage growing as the ligands become less familiar — preserves accuracy at the ligand interface, and extends its advantage when crystal backbones are replaced by predicted structures. Its designs are diverse, novel and natural in composition. An analysis of measured aptamer–target interactions shows that model likelihood scores real aptamers above random sequences but does not rank binding affinity; these results motivate the evaluation protocol we release and move aptamer design from folding to binding.

## Introduction

Aptamers are short single-stranded nucleic acids that bind molecular targets with high affinity and specificity. They are now widely used in diagnostics, targeted delivery and synthetic biology precisely because they combine this recognition with ease of chemical synthesis and directed evolution. Selections such as SELEX routinely produce RNA aptamers that bind small molecules, peptides and proteins, including targets that are difficult for other molecules. But aptamer discovery remains experimental. Selections are slow and expensive, and must be repeated for each new target. In principle, computational sequence design could shorten this search.

Inverse-folding methods design RNA sequences conditioned on a three-dimensional backbone. RhoDesign couples a geometric graph encoder with a transformer decoder, and gRNAde demonstrates multi-state design on Eterna backbones. NA-MPNN extends message-passing inverse folding to nucleic acids, and AlignIF adds evolutionary priors from structure alignments. These tools consistently improve sequence recovery, the fraction of positions matching the native sequence. But the small-molecule target is absent from all four models. Even NA-MPNN, which can condition on a protein partner, never conditions on it during training or inference. AlignIF's wet-lab experiments tested structure-only design for binding and found that improved structural fidelity left binding affinity unchanged or worse. Binding is the defining function of an aptamer, but it has never been a direct input or training objective of aptamer sequence design.

The protein field has already solved this problem. LigandMPNN demonstrated that feeding ligand atoms into a protein inverse-folding model improves sequence recovery at ligand-binding residues. For RNA, however, the same strategy is hard to follow, because the PDB contains fewer than 900 RNA–small-molecule complexes, one to two orders of magnitude fewer than the available RNA structural or SELEX-derived sequence data. A second problem is that inverse-folding methods leave secondary structure unused. RNAbpFlow recently showed that base-pairing conditioning improves RNA structure generation. For aptamers this signal is even more useful, because secondary structure frames the binding pocket. But no method has combined it with ligand context for sequence design.

Here we present TACo-Aptamer (Target-Atom-Conditioned aptamer design), an RNA inverse-folding model that conditions on the ligand and secondary structure. Its pocket atoms are embedded as tokens (element type and distances to the RNA) and modeled jointly with the nucleotide representations during finetuning on complexes, while a predicted secondary structure adds complementary constraints. To mitigate the data shortage problem, the model is first pretrained on the 5,773-chain training data and then finetuned on complexes with ligand context. Because a design method must generalize to targets it has never seen, we use a strict evaluation protocol that combines cluster-based holdout, time-based splitting and ligand holdout. And because global recovery misses interface effects, which involve only about 3% of sequence positions, we measure recovery directly at these positions. Under this protocol, TACo-Aptamer outperforms all four retrained baselines on every test set by both recovery measures and in absolute interface recovery, with advantages of 1.9–5.5% over the strongest competitor. The advantage grows as test ligands become less familiar, and persists when crystal backbones are replaced by predicted structures. Its designs remain diverse, novel and natural in composition. An analysis of 442 interactions across two public aptamer databases found that the likelihood of a structure-conditioned model ranks real SELEX aptamers higher than random sequences matched for structure but cannot rank binding affinity within families. Likelihood measures structural compatibility, which is necessary for aptamer function but cannot predict it. Binding must therefore be measured, not inferred from likelihood. TACo-Aptamer is built on this principle, both in its architecture and in its evaluation.

## Results

### Overview of TACo-Aptamer

TACo-Aptamer designs RNA sequences for a target backbone structure and conditions on two signals beyond the backbone, the atoms of the bound ligand and a predicted secondary structure (Fig. 1a). The backbone is encoded as a geometric graph over nine atoms per nucleotide and processed by a geometric vector perceptron (GVP) encoder, with the predicted secondary structure contributing pairwise edge features (Fig. 1b). Each atom of the ligand pocket is represented as a token that encodes its element type and its distances to the RNA, and these tokens are jointly attended with the nucleotide representations, so that the autoregressive transformer decoder conditions on the pocket at every decoding step. Because these tokens are generic rather than taken from a ligand-specific vocabulary, the same architecture can accommodate any ligand. Because RNA–ligand structures are scarce, training proceeds in two stages (Fig. 1c). The model is first pretrained on 5,773 RNA-only chains and then finetuned on complexes with the ligand tokens added. The final model is an ensemble of two stage-2 variants selected on validation data (Methods).

For a fair comparison, we retrained all four baselines (RhoDesign, gRNAde, NA-MPNN and AlignIF) on the same training data using their official training procedures, and evaluated every method on three test layers (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Fig. 1). Each method's official featurization pipeline tolerates slightly different chains, so the effective training sets span 5,584–5,773 of the 5,773 training chains (Extended Data Table 1). The first is a cluster-based test set of 477 chains, the second a time-based test set of 235 chains released in 2024 or later, and the third a zero-shot test set of 93 chains whose ligands never occur in any training complex. No chain in any of the three test layers occurs in the training data or the finetuning complexes. We repaired or removed data errors that had silently inflated earlier baseline results, including concatenated multi-copy crystal chains, trace protein contamination and modified-nucleotide mapping errors (Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Table 1). During benchmark inference, TACo-Aptamer conditions only on the backbone and the predicted secondary structure, exactly as the structure-only baselines do, while the ligand context reaches the model only through finetuning. The data splits, data-quality review and experimental settings are described in Methods.

### Recovery of native RNA sequences

To demonstrate the effectiveness of TACo-Aptamer, we first compared all five methods on the cluster-based test set (Figs. 1e and 2a,b). TACo-Aptamer recovered 74.90% of the native sequence with its best sampled design per chain across all sampling temperatures, 5.5% higher than the strongest baseline, RhoDesign (P = 4.8 × 10⁻⁴⁸; Fig. 2c). This advantage does not come from selecting the best of a large pool. Indeed, at low temperature (t = 0.1), the average design recovers 60.40% of the native sequence, 5.3% higher than RhoDesign (P = 6.1 × 10⁻³²). Moreover, TACo-Aptamer outperforms RhoDesign on 77.1% of chains and AlignIF on 95.4% (Fig. 2d–f), so the effect is not driven by a subset of easy chains. The probability that TACo-Aptamer assigns to a design predicts how much of the native sequence the design recovers. Across chains, the mean log probability of the sampled designs correlates with recovery (Spearman ρ = 0.516, 95% CI 0.44–0.59; Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 2). Within individual chains, the probability of a design is positively correlated with its recovery for 84% of chains, and selecting the top 10% of designs by probability raises mean recovery by 1.46%, a simple basis for ranking designs within large pools. The advantage over every baseline is significant on every test set under both measures (largest P = 5.1 × 10⁻⁵; Supplementary Table 2) and remains consistent across chain lengths (Fig. 2h). TACo-Aptamer also assigns the highest likelihood to the native sequences themselves, with a perplexity of 2.91 against 3.02–3.45 for the three baselines that can score them (Extended Data Table 2; Supplementary Fig. 3), so the recovery advantage does not depend on a distorted sequence distribution.

The advantage is uniform across nucleotide types and does not arise from bias in base composition. At t = 0.1, per-nucleotide accuracy spans 0.51 (A) to 0.70 (G), and the macro-averaged F1 score over the four bases is 0.577 (95% CI 0.567–0.586) against 0.313–0.513 for the baselines (Extended Data Table 2). Guanine appears 13% more often in TACo-Aptamer's designs than in the native sequences, while gRNAde predicts uracil correctly at only 11.8% of native uracil positions. The advantage also holds across very different ligand families. When the cluster-based test set is stratified by ligand family, TACo-Aptamer outperforms RhoDesign in eight of the nine annotated families (Extended Data Table 3; Supplementary Fig. 4), including purine riboswitches (+5.2%, P = 1.1 × 10⁻¹²), theophylline aptamers (+7.7%, P = 2.4 × 10⁻⁴), xanthine riboswitches (+8.3%, P = 0.016) and ZTP riboswitches (+7.7%, P = 7.8 × 10⁻³). Finally, not every inverse-folding model provides a usable confidence signal. The log probabilities that AlignIF and NA-MPNN assign to their designs rank chains at least as well as TACo-Aptamer's (ρ = 0.68 for both), but neither model ranks designs within a chain. AlignIF's within-chain correlations average ρ = 0.01, and selecting its most confident 10% of designs lowers recovery by 0.1%. NA-MPNN's official inference draws only 10 samples per chain, too few for within-chain ranking. RhoDesign's probabilities rank neither chains (ρ = 0.17) nor samples (mean ρ = 0.02), and selecting the top 10% of its designs enriches recovery by only +0.04% (not significant). TACo-Aptamer is informative at both levels, which is what makes the screening of large design pools possible.

### Zero-shot generalisation to unseen ligands

To test whether ligand-aware training improves or degrades performance when the ligand is new, we evaluated all methods on the zero-shot test set (Fig. 3a; Supplementary Note 2, Supplementary Table 3). TACo-Aptamer remains first by both recovery measures, with 76.06% for the best sample per chain and 62.82% at t = 0.1 against RhoDesign's 71.75% and 59.46% (Δ = +4.3% and +3.4%, P = 7.2 × 10⁻⁹ and P = 4.7 × 10⁻⁵; Fig. 3b–d), and likewise on the time-based test set (74.44% and 58.25%; Δ = +3.2% and +1.9%; Fig. 3e–g). The zero-shot advantage is comparable to that on the cluster-based test set, indicating that ligand-aware finetuning generalises beyond the training ligands, and that what the model learns about the interface transfers to pockets whose ligands were never seen.

We tested this conclusion in two further ways. First, the advantage over RhoDesign tends to decrease as the test ligand becomes more similar to the training ligands (Extended Data Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. 5). When each zero-shot chain is scored by the maximum Tanimoto similarity of its ligand to the 247 training ligands, the advantage over RhoDesign correlates negatively with similarity (Spearman ρ = −0.40 for the t = 0.1 mean, P = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴; ρ = −0.25 for the best sample, P = 0.02). The advantage is +4.6% in the similarity range 0.2–0.4 (n = 43 chains, P = 5.4 × 10⁻⁵) and +6.5% in the range 0.4–0.6 (n = 13, P = 1.5 × 10⁻³), and even the most familiar third of ligands (similarity ≥ 0.6) retains a +2.2% advantage (n = 27, P = 0.022). The negative correlation also holds with two other fingerprints, MACCS and topological (ρ = −0.34 to −0.35 for the t = 0.1 mean, P ≤ 1.3 × 10⁻³; Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 4), so it does not depend on the choice of similarity measure. Second, leave-one-family-out retraining shows that the zero-shot advantage does not depend on any single dominant family (Extended Data Fig. 2; Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). Removing any of the five largest finetuning families lowers zero-shot recovery. The aminoglycoside family (128 chains) has the largest effect, a 1.9% drop (P = 2.7 × 10⁻⁴), while a control retrained on the same complexes with no family removed changes the score by only 0.04%. Training on just two families (24% of the finetuning chains) already reproduces the zero-shot score of the model trained on all complexes to within 0.1%. Ligand conditioning is thus a transferable, chemically additive signal rather than a reliance on the few ligands that dominate the finetuning set.

### Ligand interface and foldability of designed sequences

Global recovery is insensitive to interface effects. Residues within 5 Å of the ligand occupy only about 3% of sequence positions in the benchmark, so even a perfect interface mechanism would change global recovery by about 1%. We therefore analysed the interface in two ways. First, component ablations (Fig. 4a) show that removing the two-model ensemble lowers recovery by 3.2% globally and 4.3% at the interface, and that within the family of single models, removing conditioning on the predicted secondary structure, augmentation or ligand context each lowers recovery by 0.6–0.8% globally, with conditioning on the predicted secondary structure the largest (Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Table 7). Second, we measured interface recovery directly (Fig. 4b,c). TACo-Aptamer recovers 59.6% of interface positions against 55.4% for RhoDesign (+4.0%, P = 0.18; Fig. 4b; Supplementary Fig. 6). At positions within 5 Å of the ligand, accuracy relative to each chain's own mean drops by 7.7–11.2% for three of the four baselines and by only 1.8% for TACo-Aptamer, with RhoDesign unchanged at +0.3% (Fig. 4c).

Recovery measures how closely a design matches the native sequence, not whether the design folds into the intended structure. The designed sequences were therefore refolded with predictors of secondary and tertiary structure. For each design, we compared the refolded secondary structure with the input pairing map (interaction network fidelity, INF; Fig. 4d). gRNAde ranks first at 0.704, because its graph features produce mutations that preserve pairing patterns even when sequence identity to native is low. Among designs below 40% identity to native, gRNAde still scores 0.68, where TACo-Aptamer and RhoDesign score 0.13–0.21. TACo-Aptamer scores 0.281 against 0.248 for RhoDesign, with 1.0 for the native sequences and 0.03 for random sequences. Recovery and INF thus vary independently across methods and are complementary criteria rather than a single summary score. Structural self-consistency, with designs refolded by RhoFold+ and compared with the native structure (Fig. 4e), confirms the same independence. The native sequence sets an upper bound of 0.466 on the self-consistency TM score (scTM; 48% pass TM ≥ 0.45), NA-MPNN designs rank first in self-consistency (0.325), gRNAde performs as well as TACo-Aptamer (0.265 against 0.263) and TACo-Aptamer ranks first in recovery. TACo-Aptamer's designs still score 3.8% higher than a random control matched for recovery (CI 2.4–5.1, P = 4 × 10⁻⁶; Fig. 4f). The same independence holds for a base-pairing contact score (self-consistency Matthews correlation coefficient, scMCC), where gRNAde (0.472) and NA-MPNN (0.463) again take the top two places, and TACo-Aptamer (0.344) scores far higher than the random control matched for recovery (0.200; P = 4.7 × 10⁻¹⁰; Supplementary Fig. 7). The random control matched for recovery (0.226 scTM) scores lower than every learning-based method, demonstrating that recovered sequence content contains fold information beyond what composition alone explains.

### Sequence design from predicted structures

In practice, the crystal backbone of an RNA–ligand complex is rarely available, so we evaluated every method on predicted backbones (Extended Data Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. 8, Supplementary Table 8). For 193 test chains spanning the full range of self-consistency quality, we replaced the crystal backbone with the RhoFold+ prediction of the native sequence and reapplied all five methods under the identical protocol, keeping the predicted secondary structure as the conditioning input. The method ranking is unchanged from the crystal-backbone setting, while the absolute advantage between the top two methods widens. TACo-Aptamer remains first by both measures, with 68.9% for the best sample per chain and 51.8% at t = 0.1, against 61.4% and 48.0% for RhoDesign, the strongest baseline. gRNAde, AlignIF and NA-MPNN follow, with recoveries of 50.7%, 41.9% and 35.3% for the best sample per chain and 35.0%, 36.8% and 29.6% at t = 0.1. The advantage over the strongest baseline widens from 5.5% on crystal backbones to 7.6%. The switch from crystal to predicted backbones lowers the recovery of the best sample per chain by 5.3% for TACo-Aptamer (P = 1.9 × 10⁻¹⁸), comparable to RhoDesign's 6.9% (P = 5.3 × 10⁻²⁴) but far smaller than AlignIF's 15.7% and NA-MPNN's 19.1%. The methods also differ in how recovery degrades with backbone quality. AlignIF's recovery on predicted backbones correlates with backbone quality. The Spearman correlation between its per-chain recovery at t = 0.1 and the predicted backbone's scTM against the native structure is ρ = 0.67 (P = 1.4 × 10⁻²⁶). TACo-Aptamer's dependence on backbone quality is much weaker (ρ = 0.22), consistent with the coordinate noise applied to backbones during its training, and the other baselines are similarly weak (|ρ| ≤ 0.23). One limitation appears when predicted backbones are combined with zero-shot ligands. Among the 32 zero-shot chains within the 193, the advantage over RhoDesign on the best sample holds (+6.7%, P = 4.8 × 10⁻⁶) but the advantage on the t = 0.1 mean disappears (P = 0.42), because TACo-Aptamer's low-temperature recovery drops more than RhoDesign's on these chains when the backbone is predicted (11.0% against 6.3%; Supplementary Fig. 8). TACo-Aptamer is also the cheapest per design among the learning-based methods, with a 300-sample pool taking about 2.8 s per chain on a single GPU (0.6–2.1 GB peak memory), roughly 50-fold faster per sample than AlignIF (Extended Data Table 4; Supplementary Fig. 9, Supplementary Table 9).

### Diversity, novelty and naturalness of designed sequences

Beyond recovery, a design method should sample a diverse set of sequences for each target. At t = 0.1, the mean pairwise edit distance among TACo-Aptamer's samples is 0.236, 2.9 times RhoDesign's 0.082, and 88.2% of its samples are unique sequences, against 29.8% for RhoDesign, so most of RhoDesign's samples are duplicates. TACo-Aptamer provides this diversity while its recovery remains 5% higher. Increasing the sampling temperature raises diversity further while lowering recovery by only 1.2% (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Fig. 10). The designs are dissimilar to the training sequences. Median maximum identity to the training library is 0.48, and only 2.1% of designs exceed 0.8 identity (Fig. 5b). The distance to known SELEX aptamers in AptaDB is similar, with a median of about 0.50. For 60 test chains, an embedding of sequence composition places the designs of all methods among native sequences rather than in separate clusters (Fig. 5c), and the 4-mer profiles within each ligand family are matched most closely by TACo-Aptamer, with a KL divergence of 3.73 against 4.31–4.66 for the baselines (Fig. 5d). A random-sequence reference reaches a KL of 3.47, and we include it as a control, since its value reflects the high entropy of random sequences rather than resemblance to any family profile. The folding energy of the designs is closest to native statistics among all methods, with an MFE per nucleotide of −0.258 kcal/mol against the native −0.254, and GC content is slightly higher than native, 0.57 compared with 0.52 (Fig. 5e).

### Fold and pocket recovery and an analysis of aptamer–target interactions

To survey diverse ligands, we examined twelve test chains of 22–119 nt, each with a different ligand, spanning purine bases, metabolite cofactors, a nucleotide triphosphate, an aminoglycoside and fluorophores (Fig. 6a). The best TACo-Aptamer sample for each chain was refolded by RhoFold+ and superposed on the native crystal chain. RhoFold+ refolding reaches scTM ≥ 0.4 on five of the twelve chains for TACo-Aptamer and on one for RhoDesign. On these chains, the redesigned sequences reproduce the native tertiary fold and the residues lining the pocket track the ligand. Across the twelve chains, TACo-Aptamer outperforms RhoDesign on the best sample for 10 of the 12 chains, with median recovery of 74% against 68%. The refolded designs show the same pattern, with higher scTM on 10 of the 12 chains and one equal score, at a median of 0.26 against 0.18. RhoDesign recovers more on the remaining two chains (6PRV_D and 6E8S_B). On 6PRV_D, RhoDesign is higher on recovery while TACo-Aptamer is higher on scTM, consistent with the independence of the two measures in Fig. 4e.

We next examined the N618-binding fluorescent RNA aptamer deposited in June 2024 (Fig. 6b,c). Three chains (8HZD_A/B and 8HZE_A) belong to both the time-based and zero-shot test sets, with their structures and the N618 fluorophore series unseen in training. The GDP and GTP moieties in these entries are covalent 5′ caps, not the functional ligand. On 8HZD_A, TACo-Aptamer recovers 86.1% of the native sequence, against 80.6% for the strongest baseline. On 8HZE_A, it is one of four methods with the same best recovery of 77.1%. On 8HZD_B, RhoDesign recovers more, 80.0% against 77.1%. Across the three chains, TACo-Aptamer has the highest mean recovery, 80.1% against 77.4% for RhoDesign. TACo-Aptamer's best design on 8HZD_A also matches the native sequence at all seven positions within 5 Å of N618 (Fig. 6c).

To test whether model likelihood predicts binding, we collected 442 measured aptamer–target interactions from two independent databases, 214 from AptaDB and 228 from the UTexas aptamer database. Each aptamer was scored under its own predicted structure and secondary structure, and compared with random sequences of the same length under identical structural conditioning (Fig. 6d; Supplementary Note 4). Real SELEX aptamers score higher than their matched controls, with a mean log-probability advantage of 1.95, and the advantage is positive for every one of the 442 interactions, with means of +2.01 in AptaDB and +1.90 in UTexas. The model's likelihood therefore scores real aptamer sequences as more compatible with their structures than random sequences. Within families, however, the correlation between likelihood and Kd is near zero (19 families, mean ρ = 0.11), so likelihood separates folded aptamers from noise but does not rank binding affinity. Four further checks support the robustness of both conclusions. Scoring under predicted and experimental structures differs by only 0.003 log probability per nucleotide, and scoring against unrelated structures removes the advantage. GC content is nearly uncorrelated with likelihood (ρ = 0.02), and excluding G-quadruplex aptamers leaves the conclusions unchanged. Both recovery and likelihood thus measure structural compatibility, a necessary condition for aptamer function, not a predictor of it. Evaluation must therefore go beyond recovery and likelihood, to interface metrics and measured binding data. Finally, when screening large design pools (Fig. 6e), the INF of each design is the strongest single ranking signal. Selecting the top 1% of designs by INF enriches recovery by 2.27%, against 1.77% with log probability, while naive averaging of the two negatively correlated rankings lowers recovery by 2.47%. Ranking designs by their true recovery reaches 14.1% at the same budget, an upper bound on what a learned combination of the two signals could achieve.

## Discussion

In this work, we presented TACo-Aptamer, an RNA inverse-folding method that conditions on the small-molecule target as atomic pocket context. Trained on fewer than 900 complexes, a two-stage model conditioned on the ligand pocket and a predicted secondary structure converts this into a consistent advantage under strict data splitting. The ligand's contribution, however, is hardly reflected in global recovery, because interface positions are only about 3% of each sequence. At interface positions, accuracy relative to each chain's mean drops by only 1.8% for TACo-Aptamer, while learning-based baselines drop by 8–11%. However, this advantage is difficult to attribute to a single module. Component ablations at pools of 300 samples do not reach significance, so ligand conditioning is implicated by the interface contrast. We then examined when the conditioning inputs influence the model (Extended Data Fig. 4; Supplementary Fig. 11). At inference, zeroing or shuffling the predicted secondary structure changes recovery by no more than 0.6%, and attention on the ligand tokens does not concentrate at the pocket. Both conditioning inputs thus influence the model during training rather than at inference. The advantage persists when the crystal backbone is replaced by a predicted structure and increases from 5.5% to 7.6%. Recovery also depends only weakly on the quality of the predicted backbone (ρ = 0.22), against 0.67 for AlignIF, the most sensitive method. When predicted backbones are combined with zero-shot ligands, the advantage on the t = 0.1 mean disappears, though the advantage on the best sample holds. Beyond recovery, the designs remain diverse, novel and natural in composition (Fig. 5).

Despite the strong performance, several limitations remain. First, the designs have not been tested in the laboratory. The analysis of 442 measured interactions shows that likelihood scores real aptamers as compatible with their structures but cannot rank their affinities. Interface conditioning therefore remains a real but limited training signal, not a binding predictor. To become a predictor, the model will need binding information in its training objective. Second, the training ligands are dominated by a few families, mainly aminoglycosides, S-adenosylmethionine and S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAM/SAH), and nucleotide triphosphates. This imbalance reflects the available structural data on RNA–small-molecule complexes, which are scarce and concentrated on a few ligand classes.

Key directions for further development include conditioning on conformational ensembles, demanded by the induced fit of aptamer binding, and negative design against related off-targets. Ranking large design pools is a more practical extension, since the difference between the best single signal and a perfect ranking remains large (Fig. 6e). Aptamer design has so far concentrated on designing sequences that fold into a given structure. This study shows that the next stage is to condition on what those structures are for, shifting the field from folding to binding.

## Methods

### Benchmark construction and data quality

All RNA-containing PDB entries (24,252 entries; downloaded August 2025 unless otherwise stated) were parsed into chain-level records; 6,988 RNA-only chains form the pool from which the training/validation/test splits are carved, and 868 RNA–small-molecule complexes were identified as finetuning candidates, of which 768 chains (510 PDB entries) form the stage-2 finetuning pool after cluster-level decontamination against the test layers (Supplementary Note 1; Supplementary Fig. 1). Functional ligands were identified by a CCD whitelist (605 ligand identities; standard buffers, ions and crystallization agents excluded), and interface residues were defined as any nucleotide atom within 5.0 Å of a ligand heavy atom.

Sequence clustering (CD-HIT-est 0.8, word size 4) and structure clustering (US-align, TM ≥ 0.45) were combined by transitive closure into 300 equivalence classes, split 8:1:1 into training (5,773 chains), validation (96) and a cluster-based test set (477); 511 chains were discarded by cluster-size and quality rules. Two additional test layers are disjoint from training by construction: an independent time-based set (235 chains first released in 2024 or later) and a zero-shot test set (93 chains, 54 from test and 39 from independent, whose ligands never occur in any training complex; the finetuning ligand set and the zero-shot ligand set have zero intersection, and finetuning chains have zero overlap with all three test sets). Thresholds follow field precedent: TM 0.45 matches the AlignIF clustering protocol and sits far above the random-pair TM average (about 0.17); CD-HIT 0.8 with -n 4 is the recommended nucleic-acid setting and closes the TM blind spot for crystals that share a sequence but adopt multiple conformations. An external contamination screen against the Eterna chemical-mapping corpora that trained RNet found prior measurements for 54% of benchmark natives but only 21% of the time-based layer, validating time-based splitting (Supplementary Note 5, Supplementary Table 10; the OpenKnot context is given in Supplementary Note 6).

A reproducibility review preceded all comparisons. Crystal chains stored as concatenated copies (40 chains, including 3 test chains) were rebuilt as single-copy all-atom PDBs and the canonical truth set (canonical_v2, 6,346 chains) was regenerated; 29 chains with trace protein contamination were removed from baseline training sets; modified nucleotides were mapped to parent bases with a documented mapping; legacy atom names were normalised; N-undetermined positions are excluded from all denominators. Official code paths required for local execution were patched, with all versions pinned: an AlignIF inference bug that invalidated 19 of 20 samples per chain on chains with numbering gaps; NA-MPNN parser failure modes (missing C1′ residues, insertion-code counting); RhoDesign NMR multi-model inputs. Five chains with missing C1′ coordinates are evaluated on valid positions only. A native-likelihood diagnostic run during benchmarking additionally exposed a vocabulary-ordering bug in our own gRNAde inference harness (sampled sequences had been written in A,C,G,U order against the model's A,G,C,U token order); every gRNAde number in this paper uses the corrected C/G-interchanged decode, which only raises gRNAde's scores relative to the erroneous decoding. All patches are documented in the released repository.

### TACo-Aptamer architecture

TACo-Aptamer is an encoder–decoder sequence designer with two conditioning channels. The first channel encodes RNA geometry: each nucleotide contributes nine atoms (C4′, C1′, C2′, C3′, N1/N9, C2, C5′, O5′, P); a geometric vector perceptron (GVP) encoder (3 layers; scalar 512, vector 256) builds node embeddings over k-nearest-neighbour edges with features invariant to the local rotation frame (dihedrals, sugar-pucker proxies), and the predicted base-pairing map contributes pairwise edge features. The second channel encodes the ligand context: every pocket atom within 5.0 Å of the RNA becomes a token, with an element one-hot plus a 16-bin radial basis embedding of its distances to nucleotide frames passed through a two-layer MLP into the model dimension. Ligand tokens are concatenated to the nucleotide sequence and jointly attended by a 3-layer transformer encoder (512 dimensions, 4 heads); a 3-layer autoregressive transformer decoder generates the sequence position by position at a chosen temperature, cross-attending the mixed context so that every decoding step sees the pocket. The architecture therefore injects atomic ligand context with no ligand-specific vocabulary: any element combination, including protein backbone atoms, is admissible without modification.

Stage 1 pretrains on the identical 5,773-chain training split, the same chains on which all four baselines are retrained, drawn from the 6,988-chain RNA-only pool (structure-to-sequence, masked-token cross-entropy, coordinate-noise and random-rotation augmentation, SS-dropout 0.15 variant retained; 60 epochs, batched by length buckets). Stage 2 finetunes on the complex pool with ligand tokens active (8 epochs, validation-selected, mixed 1:1 with stage-1 chains against forgetting). The released configuration, ens2, averages the stepwise softmax probabilities of two stage-2 checkpoints (seed 43 and SS-dropout 0.15) before multinomial sampling. Pool sizes are unchanged relative to single models (pools of equal size; inference cost ×2, reported), and the choice was made on validation data only. This is the standard ensemble-decoding operation applied to autoregressive sequence models, a linear opinion pool of the two stepwise distributions (refs. Sennrich et al.; Lakshminarayanan et al.), whose ensemble error is bounded by mean member error minus a diversity term (ref. Krogh & Vedelsby); gains therefore grow precisely when members disagree. Member selection followed a scan of development variants (Supplementary Table 11, Supplementary Note 7): random seed, SS-dropout rate, batch size, EMA and exact reruns of the released configuration, all finetuned under one identical stage-2 recipe. The scan ranked these two configurations as the strongest single models (71.49% and 71.45% for the best sample per chain under the scan protocol; all other pre-selection variants ≤ 70.95%; a combined seed-43 + SS-dropout variant trained after selection scored 71.44%, statistically indistinguishable from either member, and was not pursued); they differ in both seed and SS-dropout regularization and occupy different stage-1 basins of the loss landscape. Consistent with linear mode connectivity theory (ref. Frankle et al.), weight-space averaging across these training variants degrades or fails to help (66–71% for soups of two to four checkpoints, against 71.5% for the best single model on the test set), whereas probability-space averaging exploits the diversity (+3.2%; Fig. 4a quantifies the ensemble's contribution, and the best single model already outperforms every baseline on every test set under both measures). A three-member ensemble adding the third-ranked variant scored marginally higher on the test set (75.2% against 74.6%) but was rejected on validation (validation recovery not improved; the simpler two-member configuration preferred), and test-set numbers were not used for the final configuration choice. At benchmark inference all methods, TACo-Aptamer included, condition on the backbone and predicted secondary structure only; the ligand channel acts during finetuning, and its effect is assessed through interface metrics and the channel ablation of Fig. 4a (pocket injection at inference was separately verified to add about 1% at the interface, not significant, once the backbone is given). Ablation variants (no SS conditioning; no ligand context; no augmentation) retrain the finetuning stage identically from the same stage-1 checkpoint.

### Baselines

RhoDesign, gRNAde, NA-MPNN and AlignIF were retrained from their official training loops on the identical 5,773-chain training split with official hyperparameters and validation-based checkpoint selection (effective training-set sizes: 5,683 / 5,584 / 5,679 / 5,733, reflecting featurization tolerances; Extended Data Table 1). For inference, every method draws from pools of identical budget: three temperatures (0.1, 0.5, 1.0) × 100 sequences for TACo-Aptamer, RhoDesign and gRNAde; AlignIF (t = 0.1 × 20) and NA-MPNN (t = 0.1 × 10) follow their official inference defaults, and comparing best samples therefore mildly disadvantages them; the t = 0.1 mean is reported alongside precisely to control for pool size (Supplementary Note 8).

### Evaluation metrics and statistics

Recovery is reported in two forms: the best sample per chain (the pool member maximising identity to the native sequence; chains averaged) and the t = 0.1 mean (each sample's recovery averaged within the t = 0.1 pool, then across chains). Unknown-base (N) positions are excluded from denominators; baseline outputs shorter than native (the five NA-MPNN chains with missing C1′ atoms, AlignIF positions returned as dashes) are aligned by difflib and scored on valid positions. Interface metrics comprise interface recovery (positions within 5 Å of the ligand), the difference between interface and overall recovery (a within-method contrast, robust to the saturation of small interfaces that inflates absolute values; most benchmark interfaces span one to two residues) and per-nucleotide recovery grouped by distance to the nearest ligand atom, both absolute and normalised per chain (group mean minus chain mean).

For SS fidelity, designs are refolded with RNAfold (2.7.2) and compared to the input pairing map by INF = TP/√(NR·NP); the input map is itself the MFE prediction of the native sequence (native control INF = 1.000, verifying the self-consistency of this convention). For self-consistency, designs and native sequences are refolded with RhoFold+ (single-sequence mode; 1,149 predictions, zero failures) and scored by scTM against the native structure (US-align, TM normalised by native length); the random control matched for recovery mutates each TACo-Aptamer design away from native while preserving per-chain identity, then passes it through the identical pipeline. As an all-atom complement to scTM, scMCC scores the base-pairing contact pattern of barnaba-annotated refolded designs against the native pairing reference (160–174 chains with valid annotation per method; paired comparisons on 157–162 shared chains).

The aptamer–target interaction analysis uses 442 measured-affinity RNA interactions (AptaDB crawl, 1,350 interactions filtered to RNA/ACGU/length 15–300/deduplicated; UTexas Zenodo 8387047, 1,495 rows identically filtered), reduced by CD-HIT-est 0.8 against the full training library to 214 + 228 clean interactions; each sequence is scored by teacher-forced ensemble log probability under its own predicted structure and SS, against 10 random-sequence controls matched for length and sharing the same structure and SS.

Generation quality is assessed by diversity (mean normalised edit distance), unique fraction, maximum identity to the training library and to AptaDB (edlib Needleman–Wunsch with 4-mer prefiltering), 4-mer composition t-SNE, leave-one-out family profiles over TM clusters (KL), and GC content and MFE per nucleotide (RNAfold). Composition and confidence are summarised by per-nucleotide-type accuracy and macro-F1 over the four bases (t = 0.1 pools, per chain then averaged; 95% CI by bootstrap) and by native-sequence perplexity (teacher-forced log probability of the canonical native sequence per chain, macro-averaged over the 477 test chains; AlignIF exposes no native-scoring mode and is excluded). Ligand similarity is the maximum Tanimoto coefficient between the test ligand and the 247 training-complex ligands (RDKit Morgan fingerprints, radius 2, 2,048 bits, on CCD SMILES); zero-shot chains are stratified into similarity ranges. Leave-one-family-out variants retrain stage 2 while excluding each of the five largest ligand families in turn (16–128 chains removed), alongside cumulative subsets of families and a control retrained on all complexes to estimate protocol noise (±0.04%).

The predicted-backbone benchmark replaces the crystal backbone with the RhoFold+ prediction of the native sequence for 193 test chains (a stratified subset of the self-consistency set) and reruns all five methods under the identical inference protocol; per-chain deltas are paired against the crystal-backbone results, and backbone-quality dependence is measured as the Spearman correlation between per-chain recovery and the prediction's scTM against the native structure. Conditioning-channel diagnostics comprise inference-time perturbation of the input secondary structure (zeroed, shuffled or centroid substitution) on all 477 test chains, and ligand-token attention mass at interface and non-interface positions on the 115 interface chains with the true ligand injected at inference, with shuffled-ligand and single-model controls. Efficiency is measured as wall-clock time per chain and per sample, throughput and peak GPU memory on probe chains under the released inference settings.

Statistical comparisons use paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests per chain; 95% confidence intervals are obtained by 1,000–5,000× bootstrap; no multiple-comparison correction was applied given the confirmatory, pre-specified structure of the comparisons. Code, splits, canonical truth sets, model weights and per-chain results are released.
