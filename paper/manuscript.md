## Abstract

RNA aptamers are short RNAs that bind molecular targets. However, most existing computational aptamer design methods use only the RNA structure and no ligand information. Structural fidelity does not guarantee binding. Here we present TACo-Aptamer, an inverse-folding model that designs RNA aptamers using the tertiary structure, a predicted secondary structure and ligand pocket atoms. The model is pretrained on RNA-only structures, then finetuned on RNA–ligand complexes. We reorganize the dataset with cluster-based holdout, time-based splitting and ligand holdout, and retrain all baselines on the same data. On every test set, TACo-Aptamer achieves higher sequence recovery than every baseline, exceeding the strongest by up to 5.5% and increasing its advantage to 7.6% on predicted backbones. Beyond recovery, its designs are 2.9 times more diverse than the strongest baseline's and remain novel and natural. Finally, across 442 measured aptamer–target interactions, model likelihood scores real aptamers higher than random sequences. Overall, these results show that TACo-Aptamer can consider the molecular target and the RNA structure, so that designed sequences are more likely to bind the target.

## Introduction

Aptamers are short single-stranded nucleic acids that bind molecular targets with high affinity and specificity. They are now widely used in diagnostics, targeted delivery and synthetic biology precisely because they combine this recognition with ease of chemical synthesis and directed evolution. Selections such as SELEX routinely produce RNA aptamers that bind small molecules, peptides and proteins, including targets that are difficult for other molecules. But aptamer discovery still depends on experiments. Selections are slow and expensive, and must be repeated for each new target. In principle, computational sequence design could shorten this search.

Inverse-folding methods design RNA sequences conditioned on a three-dimensional backbone. RhoDesign couples a geometric graph encoder with a transformer decoder, and gRNAde demonstrates multi-state design on Eterna backbones. NA-MPNN extends message-passing inverse folding to nucleic acids, and AlignIF adds evolutionary priors from structure alignments. These tools consistently improve sequence recovery, the fraction of positions matching the native sequence. But the small-molecule target is absent from all four models. Even NA-MPNN, which can condition on a protein partner, never conditions on it during training or inference. AlignIF's wet-lab experiments tested structure-only design for binding and found that improved structural fidelity left binding affinity unchanged or worse. Binding is the defining function of an aptamer, but it has never been a direct input or training objective of aptamer sequence design.

The protein field already has an established solution. LigandMPNN demonstrated that feeding ligand atoms into a protein inverse-folding model improves sequence recovery at ligand-binding residues. For RNA, however, the same strategy is hard to follow, because the PDB contains fewer than 900 RNA–small-molecule complexes, one to two orders of magnitude fewer than the available RNA structural or SELEX-derived sequence data. A second problem is that inverse-folding methods leave secondary structure unused. RNAbpFlow recently showed that base-pairing conditioning improves RNA structure generation. For aptamers this signal is even more useful, because secondary structure frames the binding pocket. But no method has combined it with ligand context for sequence design.

Here we present TACo-Aptamer (Target-Atom-Conditioned aptamer design), an RNA inverse-folding model that conditions on the ligand and secondary structure. Its pocket atoms are embedded as tokens (element type and distance to the nearest nucleotide) and modelled jointly with the nucleotide representations during finetuning on complexes, while a predicted secondary structure adds complementary constraints. To mitigate the data shortage problem, the model is first pretrained on the 5,773-chain training data and then finetuned on complexes with ligand context. Because a design method must generalise to targets it has never seen, we use a strict evaluation protocol that combines cluster-based holdout, time-based splitting and ligand holdout. And because global recovery misses interface effects, which involve only about 3% of sequence positions, we measure recovery directly at these positions.

Under this protocol, TACo-Aptamer outperforms all four retrained baselines on every test set by both recovery measures and in absolute interface recovery, with advantages of 1.9–5.5% over the strongest competitor. The advantage grows as test ligands become less familiar, and widens when crystal backbones are replaced by predicted structures. Its designs remain diverse, novel and natural in composition. The probability the model assigns to its own designs predicts their recovery at both the chain and the sample level, which makes large design pools practical to screen. Inference is also roughly 50-fold faster per sample than AlignIF. An analysis of 442 interactions across two public aptamer databases found that the likelihood of a structure-conditioned model ranks real SELEX aptamers higher than random sequences matched for structure but cannot rank binding affinity within families. Likelihood measures structural compatibility, which is necessary for aptamer function, but cannot itself predict function. Binding must therefore be measured, not inferred from likelihood. TACo-Aptamer is built on this principle, both in its architecture and in its evaluation.

## Results

### Overview of TACo-Aptamer

TACo-Aptamer designs RNA sequences for a target backbone structure and conditions on two signals beyond the backbone, the atoms of the bound ligand and a predicted secondary structure (Fig. 1a). The backbone is encoded as a geometric graph over nine atoms per nucleotide and processed by a geometric vector perceptron (GVP) encoder, with the predicted secondary structure contributing pairwise edge features (Fig. 1b). Each atom of the ligand pocket is represented as a token that encodes its element type and its distances to the RNA (Fig. 1a), and these tokens are jointly attended with the nucleotide representations (Fig. 1c), so that the autoregressive transformer decoder conditions on the pocket at every decoding step (Fig. 1d). Because these tokens are generic rather than taken from a ligand-specific vocabulary, the same architecture can accommodate any ligand. Because RNA–ligand structures are scarce, training proceeds in two stages (Methods). The model is first pretrained on 5,773 RNA-only chains and then finetuned on complexes with the ligand tokens added. The final model is an ensemble of two stage-2 variants selected on validation data (Methods).

For a fair comparison, we retrained all four baselines (RhoDesign, gRNAde, NA-MPNN and AlignIF) on the same training data using their official training procedures, and evaluated every method on three test layers (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Each method's official featurization pipeline tolerates slightly different chains, so the effective training sets span 5,584–5,773 of the 5,773 training chains (Extended Data Table 1). The first is a cluster-based test set of 477 chains, the second a time-based test set of 235 chains released in 2024 or later, and the third a zero-shot test set of 93 chains whose ligands never occur in any training complex. No chain in any of the three test layers occurs in the training data or the finetuning complexes. We repaired or removed data errors that had silently inflated earlier baseline results, including concatenated multi-copy crystal chains, mislabelled protein chains and modified-nucleotide mapping errors (Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Table 1). During benchmark inference, TACo-Aptamer conditions only on the backbone and the predicted secondary structure, exactly as the structure-only baselines do, while the ligand context reaches the model only through finetuning. The data splits, data-quality review and experimental settings are described in Methods.

### Recovery of native RNA sequences

To demonstrate the effectiveness of TACo-Aptamer, we first compared all five methods on the cluster-based test set (Fig. 2a). TACo-Aptamer recovered 74.90% of the native sequence with its best sampled design per chain across all sampling temperatures, 5.5% higher than the strongest baseline, RhoDesign (P = 4.8 × 10⁻⁴⁸; Fig. 2a). This advantage does not come from selecting the best of a large pool. Indeed, at low temperature (t = 0.1), the average design recovers 60.40% of the native sequence, 5.3% higher than RhoDesign (P = 6.1 × 10⁻³²). Moreover, TACo-Aptamer outperforms RhoDesign on 77.1% of chains and AlignIF on 95.4% (Fig. 2b–d), so the effect is not driven by a subset of easy chains. The probability that TACo-Aptamer assigns to a design predicts how much of the native sequence the design recovers. Across chains, the mean log probability of the sampled designs correlates with recovery (Spearman ρ = 0.516, 95% CI 0.44–0.59; Fig. 2e–g and Supplementary Fig. 3). Within individual chains, the probability of a design is positively correlated with its recovery for 84% of chains, and selecting the top 10% of designs by probability raises mean recovery by 1.46%, a simple basis for ranking designs within large pools. The advantage over every baseline is significant on every test set under both measures (largest P = 5.1 × 10⁻⁵; Supplementary Table 2) and remains consistent across chain lengths (Fig. 2h). TACo-Aptamer also assigns the highest likelihood to the native sequences themselves, with a perplexity of 2.91 against 3.02–3.45 for the three baselines that can score them (Extended Data Table 2; Supplementary Fig. 4), so the recovery advantage does not depend on a distorted sequence distribution.

The advantage is uniform across nucleotide types and does not arise from bias in base composition. At t = 0.1, per-nucleotide accuracy spans 0.51 (A) to 0.70 (G), and the macro-averaged F1 score over the four bases is 0.577 (95% CI 0.567–0.586) against 0.313–0.513 for the baselines (Extended Data Table 2). Guanine appears 13% more often in TACo-Aptamer's designs than in the native sequences, while gRNAde predicts uracil correctly at only 11.8% of native uracil positions. The advantage also holds across very different ligand families. When the cluster-based test set is stratified by ligand family, TACo-Aptamer outperforms RhoDesign in eight of the nine annotated families (Fig. 2i and Extended Data Table 3; Supplementary Fig. 5), including purine riboswitches (+5.2%, P = 1.1 × 10⁻¹²), theophylline aptamers (+7.7%, P = 2.4 × 10⁻⁴), xanthine riboswitches (+8.3%, P = 0.016) and ZTP riboswitches (+7.7%, P = 7.8 × 10⁻³). Finally, not every inverse-folding model provides a usable confidence signal. The log probabilities that AlignIF and NA-MPNN assign to their designs rank chains at least as well as TACo-Aptamer's (ρ = 0.68 for both), but neither model ranks designs within a chain. AlignIF's within-chain correlations average ρ = 0.01, and selecting its most confident 10% of designs lowers recovery by 0.1%. NA-MPNN's official inference draws only 10 samples per chain, too few for within-chain ranking. RhoDesign's probabilities rank neither chains (ρ = 0.17) nor samples (mean ρ = 0.02), and selecting the top 10% of its designs enriches recovery by only +0.04% (not significant). gRNAde's probabilities rank chains only weakly (ρ = 0.15) and do not rank samples (mean ρ = −0.03), and selecting the top 10% of its designs lowers recovery by 0.4%. TACo-Aptamer is informative at both levels, which is what makes the screening of large design pools possible.

### Generalisation to unseen ligands and predicted backbones

To test whether ligand-aware training improves or degrades performance when the ligand is new, we evaluated all methods on the zero-shot test set (Fig. 3; Supplementary Note 2, Supplementary Table 3). TACo-Aptamer remains first by both recovery measures, with 76.06% for the best sample per chain and 62.82% at t = 0.1 against RhoDesign's 71.75% and 59.46% (Δ = +4.3% and +3.4%, P = 7.2 × 10⁻⁹ and P = 4.7 × 10⁻⁵; Fig. 3a–c), and likewise on the time-based test set (74.44% and 58.25%; Δ = +3.2% and +1.9%; Fig. 3d–f). The zero-shot advantage is comparable to that on the cluster-based test set, indicating that ligand-aware finetuning generalises beyond the training ligands, and that what the model learns about the interface transfers to pockets whose ligands were never seen.

We tested this conclusion in two further ways. First, the advantage over RhoDesign tends to decrease as the test ligand becomes more similar to the training ligands (Extended Data Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. 6). When each zero-shot chain is scored by the maximum Tanimoto similarity of its ligand to the 247 training ligands, the advantage over RhoDesign correlates negatively with similarity (Spearman ρ = −0.40 for the t = 0.1 mean, P = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴; ρ = −0.25 for the best sample, P = 0.02). The advantage of the best sample is +4.6% in the similarity range 0.2–0.4 (n = 43 chains, P = 5.4 × 10⁻⁵) and +6.5% in the range 0.4–0.6 (n = 13, P = 1.5 × 10⁻³), and even the most familiar third of ligands (similarity ≥ 0.6) retains a +2.2% advantage (n = 27, P = 0.022). The negative correlation also holds with two other fingerprints, MACCS and topological (ρ = −0.34 to −0.35 for the t = 0.1 mean, P ≤ 1.3 × 10⁻³; Extended Data Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 4), so it does not depend on the choice of similarity measure. Second, leave-one-family-out retraining shows that the zero-shot advantage does not depend on any single dominant family (Extended Data Fig. 2; Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). Removing any of the five largest finetuning families lowers zero-shot recovery. The aminoglycoside family (128 chains) has the largest effect, a 1.9% drop (P = 2.7 × 10⁻⁴), while a control retrained on the same complexes with no family removed changes the score by only 0.04%. Training on just two families (24% of the finetuning chains) already reproduces the zero-shot score of the model trained on all complexes to within 0.1%. Ligand conditioning is thus a transferable, chemically additive signal rather than a reliance on the few ligands that dominate the finetuning set.

In practice, the crystal backbone of an RNA–ligand complex is rarely available, so we evaluated every method on predicted backbones (Fig. 3g–i; Extended Data Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 7, Supplementary Table 7). For 193 test chains spanning the full range of self-consistency quality, we replaced the crystal backbone with the RhoFold+ prediction of the native sequence and reapplied all five methods under the identical protocol, keeping the predicted secondary structure as the conditioning input. The method ranking is unchanged from the crystal-backbone setting, while the absolute advantage between the top two methods widens. TACo-Aptamer remains first by both measures, with 68.9% for the best sample per chain and 51.8% at t = 0.1, against 61.4% and 48.0% for RhoDesign, the strongest baseline. gRNAde, AlignIF and NA-MPNN follow, with recoveries of 50.7%, 41.9% and 35.3% for the best sample per chain and 35.0%, 36.8% and 29.6% at t = 0.1. The advantage over the strongest baseline widens from 5.5% on crystal backbones to 7.6%. The switch from crystal to predicted backbones lowers the recovery of the best sample per chain by 5.3% for TACo-Aptamer (P = 1.9 × 10⁻¹⁸), comparable to RhoDesign's 6.9% (P = 5.3 × 10⁻²⁴) but far smaller than AlignIF's 15.7% and NA-MPNN's 19.1% (Extended Data Fig. 3). The methods also differ in how recovery degrades with backbone quality. AlignIF's recovery on predicted backbones correlates with backbone quality. The Spearman correlation between its per-chain recovery at t = 0.1 and the predicted backbone's scTM against the native structure is ρ = 0.67 (P = 1.4 × 10⁻²⁶). TACo-Aptamer's dependence on backbone quality is much weaker (ρ = 0.22), consistent with the coordinate noise applied to backbones during its training, and the other baselines are similarly weak (|ρ| ≤ 0.23). One limitation appears when predicted backbones are combined with zero-shot ligands. Among the 32 zero-shot chains within the 193, the advantage over RhoDesign on the best sample holds (+6.7%, P = 4.8 × 10⁻⁶) but the advantage on the t = 0.1 mean disappears (P = 0.42), because TACo-Aptamer's low-temperature recovery drops more than RhoDesign's on these chains when the backbone is predicted (11.0% against 6.3%; Fig. 3i, Supplementary Fig. 7). TACo-Aptamer is also the cheapest per design among the learning-based methods, with a 300-sample pool taking about 2.8 s per chain on a single GPU (0.6–2.1 GB peak memory), roughly 50-fold faster per sample than AlignIF (Extended Data Table 4; Supplementary Fig. 8, Supplementary Table 8).

### Ligand interface and foldability of designed sequences

Global recovery is insensitive to interface effects. Residues within 5 Å of the ligand occupy only about 3% of sequence positions in the benchmark, so even a perfect interface mechanism would change global recovery by about 1%. We therefore analysed the interface in two ways. First, component ablations (Fig. 4a) show that removing the two-model ensemble lowers recovery by 3.2% globally and 4.3% at the interface, and that within the family of single models, removing conditioning on the predicted secondary structure, augmentation or ligand context each lowers recovery by 0.6–0.8% globally, with conditioning on the predicted secondary structure the largest (Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Table 9). Second, we measured interface recovery directly (Fig. 4b–d). TACo-Aptamer recovers 59.6% of interface positions against 55.4% for RhoDesign (+4.0%, P = 0.18; Fig. 4b; Supplementary Fig. 9). At positions within 5 Å of the ligand, accuracy relative to each chain's own mean drops by 7.7–11.2% for three of the four baselines and by only 1.8% for TACo-Aptamer, with RhoDesign unchanged at +0.3% (Fig. 4d).

Recovery measures how closely a design matches the native sequence, not whether the design folds into the intended structure. The designed sequences were therefore refolded with predictors of secondary and tertiary structure. For each design, we compared the refolded secondary structure with the input pairing map (interaction network fidelity, INF; Fig. 4e). gRNAde ranks first at 0.704, because its graph features produce mutations that preserve pairing patterns even when sequence identity to native is low (Fig. 4f). Among designs below 40% identity to native, gRNAde still scores 0.68, where TACo-Aptamer and RhoDesign score 0.13–0.21. TACo-Aptamer scores 0.281 against 0.248 for RhoDesign, with 1.0 for the native sequences and 0.03 for random sequences. Recovery and INF thus vary independently across methods and are complementary criteria rather than a single summary score. Structural self-consistency, with designs refolded by RhoFold+ and compared with the native structure (Fig. 4g), confirms the same independence. The native sequence sets an upper bound of 0.466 on the self-consistency TM score (scTM; 48% pass TM ≥ 0.45), NA-MPNN designs rank first in self-consistency (0.325), gRNAde performs as well as TACo-Aptamer (0.265 against 0.263) and TACo-Aptamer ranks first in recovery. TACo-Aptamer's designs still score 3.8% higher than a random control matched for recovery (CI 2.4–5.1, P = 4 × 10⁻⁶; Fig. 4h). The same independence holds for a base-pairing contact score (self-consistency Matthews correlation coefficient, scMCC), where gRNAde (0.472) and NA-MPNN (0.463) again take the top two places, and TACo-Aptamer (0.344) scores far higher than the random control matched for recovery (0.200; P = 4.7 × 10⁻¹⁰; Fig. 4i; per-chain scatter in Supplementary Fig. 10). The random control matched for recovery (0.226 scTM) scores lower than every learning-based method, demonstrating that recovered sequence content contains fold information beyond what composition alone explains.

### Diversity, novelty and naturalness of designed sequences

Beyond recovery, a design method should sample a diverse set of sequences for each target. At t = 0.1, the mean pairwise edit distance among TACo-Aptamer's samples is 0.236, 2.9 times RhoDesign's 0.082, and 88.2% of its samples are unique sequences, against 29.8% for RhoDesign, so most of RhoDesign's samples are duplicates. TACo-Aptamer provides this diversity while its recovery remains 5% higher. Increasing the sampling temperature raises diversity further while lowering recovery by only 1.2% (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Fig. 11). The designs are dissimilar to the training sequences. Median maximum identity to the training library is 0.48, and only 2.1% of designs exceed 0.8 identity (Fig. 5b). The distance to known SELEX aptamers in AptaDB is similar, with a median of about 0.50 (Fig. 5c). For 60 test chains, an embedding of sequence composition places the designs of all methods among native sequences rather than in separate clusters (Fig. 5d), and the 4-mer profiles within each ligand family are matched most closely by TACo-Aptamer, with a KL divergence of 3.73 against 4.31–4.66 for the baselines (Fig. 5e). A random-sequence reference reaches a KL of 3.47, and we include it as a control, since its value reflects the high entropy of random sequences rather than resemblance to any family profile. The folding energy of the designs is closest to native statistics among all methods, with an MFE per nucleotide of −0.258 kcal/mol against the native −0.254, and GC content is slightly higher than native, 0.57 compared with 0.52 (Fig. 5f).

### Fold and pocket recovery and an analysis of aptamer–target interactions

To survey diverse ligands, we examined twelve test chains of 22–119 nt, each with a different ligand, spanning purine bases, metabolite cofactors, a nucleotide triphosphate, an aminoglycoside and fluorophores (Fig. 6a). The best TACo-Aptamer sample for each chain was refolded by RhoFold+ and superposed on the native crystal chain. RhoFold+ refolding reaches scTM ≥ 0.4 on five of the twelve chains for TACo-Aptamer and on one for RhoDesign. On these chains, the redesigned sequences reproduce the native tertiary fold and the residues lining the pocket track the ligand. Across the twelve chains, TACo-Aptamer outperforms RhoDesign on the best sample for 10 of the 12 chains, with median recovery of 74% against 68%. The refolded designs show the same pattern, with higher scTM on 10 of the 12 chains and one equal score, at a median of 0.26 against 0.18. RhoDesign recovers more on the remaining two chains (6PRV_D and 6E8S_B). On 6PRV_D, RhoDesign is higher on recovery while TACo-Aptamer is higher on scTM, consistent with the independence of the two measures in Fig. 4g.

We next examined the N618-binding fluorescent RNA aptamer deposited in June 2024 (Fig. 6b,c). Three chains (8HZD_A/B and 8HZE_A) belong to both the time-based and zero-shot test sets, with their structures and the N618 fluorophore series unseen in training. The GDP and GTP moieties in these entries are covalent 5′ caps, not the functional ligand. On 8HZD_A, TACo-Aptamer recovers 86.1% of the native sequence, against 80.6% for the strongest baseline. On 8HZE_A, it is one of four methods with the same best recovery of 77.1%. On 8HZD_B, RhoDesign recovers more, 80.0% against 77.1%. Across the three chains, TACo-Aptamer has the highest mean recovery, 80.1% against 77.4% for RhoDesign. TACo-Aptamer's best design on 8HZD_A also matches the native sequence at all seven positions within 5 Å of N618 (Fig. 6c).

To test whether model likelihood predicts binding, we collected 442 measured aptamer–target interactions from two independent databases, 214 from AptaDB and 228 from the UTexas aptamer database. Each aptamer was scored under its own predicted structure and secondary structure, and compared with random sequences of the same length under identical structural conditioning (Fig. 6d,e; Supplementary Note 4). Real SELEX aptamers score higher than their matched controls, with a mean log-probability advantage of 1.95, and the advantage is positive for every one of the 442 interactions, with means of +2.01 in AptaDB and +1.90 in UTexas. The model's likelihood therefore scores real aptamer sequences as more compatible with their structures than random sequences. Within families, however, the correlation between likelihood and Kd is near zero (19 families, mean ρ = 0.11), so likelihood separates folded aptamers from noise but does not rank binding affinity. Four further checks support the robustness of both conclusions. Scoring under predicted and experimental structures differs by only 0.003 log probability per nucleotide, and scoring against unrelated structures removes the advantage. GC content is nearly uncorrelated with likelihood (ρ = 0.02), and excluding G-quadruplex aptamers leaves the conclusions unchanged. Both recovery and likelihood thus measure structural compatibility, a necessary condition for aptamer function, not a predictor of it. Evaluation must therefore go beyond recovery and likelihood, to interface metrics and measured binding data. Finally, when screening large design pools (Fig. 6f), the INF of each design is the strongest single ranking signal. Selecting the top 1% of designs by INF enriches recovery by 2.27%, against 1.77% with log probability, while naive averaging of the two negatively correlated rankings lowers recovery by 2.47%. Ranking designs by their true recovery reaches 14.1% at the same budget, an upper bound on what a learned combination of the two signals could achieve.

## Discussion

In this work, we presented TACo-Aptamer, an RNA inverse-folding method that conditions on the small-molecule target as atomic pocket context. Trained on fewer than 900 complexes, a two-stage model conditioned on the ligand pocket and a predicted secondary structure converts this into a consistent advantage under strict data splitting. The ligand's contribution, however, is hardly reflected in global recovery, because interface positions are only about 3% of each sequence. At interface positions, accuracy relative to each chain's mean drops by only 1.8% for TACo-Aptamer, while learning-based baselines drop by 8–11%. However, this advantage is difficult to attribute to a single module. Component ablations at pools of 300 samples do not reach significance, so ligand conditioning is implicated by the interface contrast. We then examined when the conditioning inputs influence the model (Extended Data Fig. 4; Supplementary Fig. 12). At inference, zeroing or shuffling the predicted secondary structure changes recovery by no more than 0.6%, and attention on the ligand tokens does not concentrate at the pocket. Both conditioning inputs thus influence the model during training rather than at inference. The advantage persists when the crystal backbone is replaced by a predicted structure and increases from 5.5% to 7.6%. Recovery also depends only weakly on the quality of the predicted backbone (ρ = 0.22), against 0.67 for AlignIF, the most sensitive method. When predicted backbones are combined with zero-shot ligands, the advantage on the t = 0.1 mean disappears, though the advantage on the best sample holds. Beyond recovery, the designs remain diverse, novel and natural in composition (Fig. 5).

Despite the strong performance, several limitations remain. First, the designs have not been tested in the laboratory. The analysis of 442 measured interactions shows that likelihood scores real aptamers as compatible with their structures but cannot rank their affinities. Interface conditioning therefore remains a real but limited training signal, not a binding predictor. To become a predictor, the model will need binding information in its training objective. Second, the training ligands are dominated by a few families, mainly aminoglycosides, S-adenosylmethionine and S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAM/SAH), and nucleotide triphosphates. This imbalance reflects the available structural data on RNA–small-molecule complexes, which are scarce and concentrated on a few ligand classes.

Key directions for further development include conditioning on conformational ensembles, demanded by the induced fit of aptamer binding, and negative design against related off-targets. Ranking large design pools is a more practical extension, since the difference between the best single signal and a perfect ranking remains large (Fig. 6f). Aptamer design has so far concentrated on designing sequences that fold into a given structure. This study shows that the next stage is to condition on what those structures are for, shifting the field from folding to binding.

## Methods

### Benchmark construction and data quality

All 24,252 PDB entries containing RNA were downloaded in August 2025 and decomposed into records for individual chains. Of these, 6,988 RNA-only chains serve as the pool for training and evaluation. In addition, 868 complexes in which an RNA chain binds a small molecule were selected as candidates for finetuning. After excluding candidates that share a cluster with any chain held out for testing, 768 chains across 510 entries form the stage-2 finetuning pool (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Functional ligands are those on a curated list of 605 distinct ligands from the PDB Chemical Component Dictionary (CCD), which excludes buffers, ions and crystallization additives. An interface residue is any nucleotide with an atom within 5.0 Å of a ligand heavy atom.

Sequence clustering (CD-HIT-est, sequence identity 0.8, word size 4) and structure clustering (US-align, TM ≥ 0.45) were combined by transitive closure into 300 equivalence classes, which were split 8:1:1 at the class level into training (5,773 chains), validation (96 chains) and a test set (477 chains). Two additional test layers share no chains with the training set by construction. One is an independent test set of 235 chains first released in 2024 or later. The other is a zero-shot test set of 93 chains whose ligands never occur in any training complex. No ligand appears in both the finetuning pool and the zero-shot set, and no finetuning chain appears in any of the three test sets. The largest class, a transfer RNA class of 5,373 chains, is retained whole in training except for its 511 members released in 2024 or later. Training contains nothing released in 2024 or later, and these chains would have close homologues in training whichever evaluation layer they joined, so they remain unassigned. Both thresholds follow established practice. The TM threshold of 0.45 matches the value used by AlignIF for clustering and is well above the average TM between random pairs of chains (about 0.17). The CD-HIT-est settings are those recommended for nucleic acids, and the sequence criterion catches crystals that share a sequence but occur in different conformations, pairs that structure clustering alone can separate. An external contamination screen of the Eterna chemical mapping corpora on which RNet was trained found previous measurements for 54% of the benchmark's native sequences but for only 21% of the independent test set, supporting the split by release date (Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Table 10). The OpenKnot context is given in Supplementary Note 6.

The benchmark inputs were reviewed for reproducibility before any comparison. In the source data, 40 crystal chains (including 3 test chains) appeared as tandem concatenations of a single molecule. These were rebuilt as single-copy all-atom PDBs, and the canonical set of native sequences (6,346 chains) was regenerated under the same logic. Twenty-nine chains listed as RNA in the source pool were in fact protein backbone traces and were removed from the baseline training sets. Modified nucleotides were mapped to their unmodified bases under a released mapping, and atom names in older formats were standardised (O5* to O5′). Positions whose native base is undetermined are excluded from every denominator. All repairs and code patches are documented in the released repository (Supplementary Note 1).

### TACo-Aptamer architecture

TACo-Aptamer is an encoder–decoder sequence designer with two conditioning inputs. The first input is the RNA geometry. Each nucleotide contributes nine atoms (C4′, C1′, C2′, C3′, N1/N9, C2, C5′, O5′, P). A GVP encoder (3 layers, 512 scalar and 256 vector channels) computes node embeddings over edges to each nucleotide's k nearest neighbours from rotation-invariant torsion angle features, and the predicted secondary structure contributes pairwise edge features. The second input is the ligand context. Every pocket atom within 5.0 Å of the RNA becomes a token, featurized by an element one-hot and a Gaussian radial basis expansion (16 basis functions over 0–20 Å) of its distance to the nearest nucleotide C1′ atom, then mapped to the model dimension by a two-layer MLP. Ligand tokens are concatenated to the nucleotide sequence and jointly attended by a 3-layer transformer encoder (512 dimensions, 4 heads). A 3-layer autoregressive transformer decoder then generates the sequence one nucleotide at a time, attending to the encoded nucleotides and ligand tokens at every step. The architecture therefore provides atomic ligand context with no ligand-specific vocabulary. Any element combination, including protein backbone atoms, can be used without modifying the model.

Stage 1 pretrains on the training split of 5,773 chains, the same chains on which all four baselines are retrained. The objective is cross-entropy on the native sequence given the structure, with Gaussian coordinate noise and random rotations as augmentation. Training runs for 60 epochs, with batches grouped by chain length. A variant that drops the secondary-structure input with probability 0.15 is retained for the final ensemble. Stage 2 finetunes the model on the 768 complex chains, with ligand tokens in the input. Finetuning runs for 8 epochs, with the checkpoint chosen on validation recovery. Each batch is mixed 1:1 with stage-1 chains to prevent forgetting. The released configuration averages the stepwise softmax probabilities of two stage-2 checkpoints before sampling. Pool sizes match those of single models. Inference costs twice as much per sample (Extended Data Table 4), and the ensemble was selected on validation data only. Averaging the probabilities before sampling is the standard ensemble technique for autoregressive sequence models. The ensemble error is bounded by the average error of the members minus their diversity, so the advantage is largest when the two members disagree. A scan of development variants (Supplementary Table 11, Supplementary Note 7) ranked the two ensemble members — the standard variant (the base recipe retrained with a different random seed) and the secondary-structure-dropout-0.15 variant — as the strongest single models, at 71.49% and 71.45% for the best sample per chain. The two were finetuned from different stage-1 checkpoints. Consistent with linear mode connectivity theory, averaging the weights of two to four checkpoints lowered recovery to 66–71% on the test set, against 71.5% for the best single model. Averaging the output probabilities instead exploits the diversity between the members and adds 3.2% (Fig. 4a). Adding the variant ranked third in the scan gave a slightly higher score on the test set (75.2% against 74.6%), but validation recovery did not improve, so the pair was retained. The test scores did not influence this decision. During benchmark inference, all five methods condition on the backbone and the predicted secondary structure only. The ligand input is used during finetuning only, and its effect is assessed through the interface metrics and the input ablation of Fig. 4a. Ablation variants (no SS conditioning; no ligand context; no augmentation) retrain the finetuning stage identically from the same stage-1 checkpoint.

### Baselines

RhoDesign, gRNAde, NA-MPNN and AlignIF were retrained on the same training split of 5,773 chains using the official procedures and hyperparameters of each method, with checkpoint selection on validation. Their featurization pipelines accept slightly different chains, so the effective training sets are 5,683 chains for RhoDesign, 5,584 for gRNAde, 5,679 for NA-MPNN and 5,733 for AlignIF (Extended Data Table 1). For inference, TACo-Aptamer, RhoDesign and gRNAde each sample 100 sequences at each of three temperatures (0.1, 0.5 and 1.0), giving pools of 300 sequences per chain. AlignIF and NA-MPNN follow their official inference defaults, sampling 20 and 10 sequences at t = 0.1. Comparing best samples slightly disadvantages these two smaller pools, so the t = 0.1 mean is also reported, as it is insensitive to pool size (Supplementary Note 8).

### Evaluation metrics and statistics

Recovery is reported for the best sample per chain and for the t = 0.1 mean. The best sample is the pool member closest to the native sequence, and these per-chain values are averaged over all chains. The t = 0.1 mean averages each sample's recovery within the t = 0.1 pool of a chain, then averages these per-chain means over all chains. Baseline outputs shorter than the native sequence are aligned by equal-block matching and scored on valid positions. This applies to AlignIF positions without a predicted nucleotide and to the five NA-MPNN chains that lack C1′ atoms. Interface recovery is the recovery of positions within 5 Å of the ligand. Because most benchmark interfaces span one to two residues, a single position dominates their absolute recovery, so the difference between interface and overall recovery is used as a contrast computed within each method. Per-nucleotide recovery is also grouped by distance to the nearest ligand atom. Group means are reported both raw and after subtracting each chain's overall mean.

For secondary-structure fidelity, designs are refolded with RNAfold (2.7.2) and compared with the input pairing map by INF, the number of correctly predicted base pairs divided by the geometric mean of the numbers of predicted and native base pairs. The input pairing map is obtained by folding the native sequence with RNAfold, so the native sequence reproduces it exactly and scores INF = 1.000. For self-consistency, designs and native sequences are refolded with RhoFold+ in single-sequence mode and scored by scTM against the native structure (US-align, with TM normalised by the length of the native chain). A random control matched for recovery mutates each TACo-Aptamer design away from the native sequence while preserving per-chain identity and passes it through the same pipeline. As an all-atom complement to scTM, scMCC scores the base pairs annotated by barnaba in each refolded design against the native base pairs.

The aptamer–target interaction analysis covers 442 measured interactions, 214 from AptaDB and 228 from the UTexas aptamer database (Supplementary Note 4). Sequences were filtered for measured affinity, valid RNA and length, and clustered against the training library to exclude leakage. Each sequence is scored by the log probability that the ensemble assigns to it under its own predicted structure and secondary structure, against 10 random sequences matched for length and predicted structure.

Generation quality is measured by diversity (mean normalised edit distance), unique fraction, maximum identity to the training library and to AptaDB (edlib alignment), 4-mer composition t-SNE, leave-one-out family profiles over TM clusters (KL divergence), GC content and MFE per nucleotide (RNAfold). Accuracy is computed for each of the four bases, together with macro-F1, on t = 0.1 pools and averaged over chains. Perplexity is computed per chain from the log probability of the canonical native sequence and macro-averaged over the 477 test chains. AlignIF cannot score native sequences and is excluded from perplexity. Ligand similarity is the maximum Tanimoto coefficient between the test ligand and the 247 training ligands (Morgan fingerprints on CCD SMILES), and zero-shot chains are stratified into similarity ranges. Leave-one-family-out variants retrain stage 2 excluding each of the five largest ligand families in turn, with cumulative subsets and a retrain-all control (Supplementary Note 2).

For 193 test chains, the crystal backbone is replaced by the RhoFold+ prediction of the native sequence and all five methods are rerun unchanged. Per-chain recovery is compared with the value on the crystal backbone, and the differences are analysed as paired values. The dependence of recovery on backbone quality is measured as the Spearman correlation between per-chain recovery and the prediction's scTM. The secondary-structure input is perturbed at inference (zeroed, shuffled or replaced by a centroid) on all 477 test chains. Attention on ligand tokens is measured at interface and non-interface positions on the 115 interface chains (Supplementary Note 3). Efficiency is measured as runtime per chain and per sample, throughput and peak GPU memory on six chains per method (Extended Data Table 4).

Statistical comparisons use paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests per chain, and 95% confidence intervals are obtained by bootstrap. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied. The comparisons were confirmatory and planned in advance.
